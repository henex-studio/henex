ReKraft

A mobile platform for sharing, trading and learning upcycling, grounded in — the Māori principle of reciprocal guardianship over people, materials and place.

View Prototype Back to Works

Overview

A values-anchored app that turns reuse intent into community action.

People want to reduce waste and reuse materials. The intent is consistent. The behaviour rarely follows. Mainstream platforms offer either commerce or inspiration in isolation, and none of them connect practical reuse to the relational ethic that makes the habit durable. ReKraft addresses this gap by combining three linked modules: Community for sharing ideas and outcomes, Market for trading and gifting materials, and for short learning missions rooted in guardianship values.

The project aligns values with (Responsible Consumption and Production). The design argument is that cultural framing is not a visual layer. It is a motivation architecture. When people understand that their actions participate in a living system of care rather than a transaction, the behaviour changes.

Background

A dream grounded in research and shaped into a cultural story.

As emerging UX designers, we wanted to respond to global sustainability challenges with empathy and cultural understanding rather than technology alone. Guided by SDG 12, we explored New Zealand's throwaway culture and found that people want to live more sustainably but often lack the time, tools and support. Rooted in Kaitiakitanga — guardianship and care for the sky, land and sea — we used this value as the cultural foundation for everyday sustainable practice.

Problem

How might we empower individuals and communities to reduce waste by creatively reusing materials, in ways that reflect the values of Kaitiakitanga?

Solution

A values-anchored, community upcycling app.

A mobile app grounded in Kaitiakitanga and SDG 12, organised around three linked modules — Community (share DIY practices and inspiration), Marketplace (trade, buy, swap or share items and materials with origin and impact cues and a Koin value mechanism), and Ako (missions and micro-quizzes that teach guardianship principles) — to turn guardianship into concrete micro-actions supported by social motivation and transparent impact.

Outcome

Tangible guardianship in practice.

The project delivered a culturally grounded concept and hi-fi prototype showing how people can move fluidly between sharing ideas, obtaining materials, making and repairing, exchanging output, and learning values, while tracking their contributions. The result demonstrates tangible waste reduction behaviours, stronger community participation, and a respectful operationalisation of Kaitiakitanga in contemporary UX.

Research

Finding out where motivation actually breaks down.

Discovery started with a deliberate question: what does Kaitiakitanga demand of a digital product?

Literature review

Guardianship is relational, not transactional.

Te Ao Māori frames guardianship as reciprocal care, not a one-off act. SDG 12 sets the global policy direction. Neither translates into interaction design without knowing where reuse actually breaks down.

Platform benchmarking

No tool holds the full picture.

Pinterest, Facebook Marketplace, Depop and repair café networks each solve one dimension. None hold community reciprocity, cultural meaning and practical reuse literacy together.

Research synthesis

Six findings, six insights.

Practical barriers and motivation gaps reinforce each other. The insights redirected the design toward activation over education.

Finding 01

Intent exists without a path.

People want to upcycle but lack the tools, skills and community support to act consistently.

Finding 02

Repurposing potential is invisible.

Most people do not see creative reuse potential in everyday waste without a prompt or example.

Finding 03

Trust and provenance both matter.

The origin of materials and the real impact of actions shape whether people participate and return.

Finding 04

Cultural values are absent from mainstream tools.

No existing platform connects reuse to a relational ethic, which weakens long-term meaning.

Finding 05

Community sustains behaviour better than tips.

Social recognition and shared momentum outperform one-off informational nudges.

Finding 06

Making can generate its own waste.

Craft and DIY processes often produce offcuts and discards that extend the circularity problem.

Implication 01

Design for the first step, not the informed user.

Implication 02

Surface ideas before asking people to act on them.

Implication 03

Make impact visible, not assumed.

Implication 04

Embed values through interaction, not decoration.

Implication 05

Design for contribution, not just consumption.

Implication 06

Close the loop by including material sharing in the making flow.

Influence

This redirected the entire design strategy from education to activation: lower the first step, make community visible, surface provenance and impact, and embed Māori values through action rather than aesthetics.

Design

Turning research into a focused design scope.

With six findings in hand, ideation surveyed adjacent directions, including a DIY reuse platform, energy sharing and trail-based cultural learning, before converging on a waste-to-treasure community platform for the strongest alignment of SDG 12, feasibility and user appeal. Development then translated the six principles into a three-module product structure, refining flows around publishing, discovery, filtering and missions.

Community

Sharing and co-creating

Addresses the isolation barrier from the research: community sustains behaviour better than tips.

Market

Trading and gifting, with origin and impact cues

Addresses the trust barrier: the origin of materials and the real impact of actions shape whether people participate.

Ako

Short learning missions tied to guardianship values

Addresses the values barrier: cultural values are absent from mainstream reuse tools.

Influence

Each module addresses a different barrier from the research without duplicating what the others do, which kept the MVP scope from turning into three copies of the same feature.

Develop

From architecture to testable prototype.

The concept diagram anchors everything that follows: three linked modules inside a Kaitiakitanga and SDG 12 frame, with Koin as the connecting mechanism.

Prototyping moved from paper sketches to lo-fi flows to mid-fidelity screens. Each stage had a specific job. Sketches validated navigation and entry points. Lo-fi flows established content hierarchy and tested whether the three modules felt distinct but connected.

Influence

Testing whether the three modules felt distinct but connected directly shaped the final IA — confirming that Community, Market and Ako needed shared navigation but separate content rhythms.

Design System

A visual language that carries values, not just aesthetics.

The design system was not built after the content. It was developed alongside it, because the visual language needed to express Kaitiakitanga rather than decorate it. Every colour, type and illustration decision was tested against the question: does this feel like guardianship, or does it feel like greenwashing?

Deep greens that signal growth, not trend.

The palette centres on forest and teal greens drawn from natural ecosystems rather than brand conventions. The dark green grounds the interface with stability, the mid teal carries interactive and action states, and secondary tones support hierarchy without competing for attention.

Seravek and Roboto for warmth and clarity.

Seravek carries headlines with a rounded, approachable quality that suits community content, while Roboto handles body text and interface labels at smaller sizes. The pairing avoids the coldness of purely technical typefaces while maintaining clear reading hierarchy across a dense information environment.

Hand-drawn quality that signals human making.

Illustrations use a loose, crafted style that references the act of making by hand, a deliberate choice against polished stock imagery, which would undermine the platform's claim that reuse is accessible.

A consistent weight system for clarity.

Icons follow a consistent weight system for navigational and action states, keeping recognisability and legibility across small touch targets on mobile.

A mark that reads as cycle and community.

The ReKraft wordmark pairs with a circular mark that references both the recycling loop and the koru, a Māori symbol of growth and new beginnings. The identity needed to work across a mobile interface and physical touchpoints without losing legibility or cultural resonance at small sizes.

Influence

This question filtered every visual decision that followed — pushing the palette toward natural greens, the illustrations toward hand-drawn warmth, and the brand mark toward symbolism rather than decoration.

Validation

Testing whether the three modules held together in practice.

Five participants completed a moderated usability test of the ReKraft prototype. The test measured three things: whether people could complete a core task in each module, where navigation and content discovery broke down, and whether Kaitiakitanga and SDG 12 came through clearly in the design rather than staying implicit.

Task 01 · Market

List an item for exchange

Given an old wooden chair to sell, participants used ReKraft to publish the relevant product information.

Task 02 · Community

Find reuse guidance

Given unused glass jars, participants used ReKraft to find relevant information on what to do with them.

Task 03 · Ako

Complete a values quiz

Participants located and completed a short quiz about Kaitiakitanga inside the Ako module.

Influence

Pre- and post-test questions checked participants' prior engagement with sustainability topics and, afterward, whether the social features helped them find what they needed, whether the learning module met expectations, and where the app still felt confusing. This shaped the Delivery-stage refinements to onboarding, module navigation and the Ako quiz flow.

Delivery

Each module earns its place.

The three modules were not designed as features. They were designed as answers to specific research findings. The community feed answers the isolation problem. The market answers the access and trust problem. Ako answers the skills and meaning problem. Together they form a loop rather than a list.

Onboarding

Values first, features second.

The onboarding flow was one of the last things designed and one of the most considered. The decision to lead with a Kaitiakitanga story rather than a feature tour was deliberate. Users who understand the platform's purpose before they see its features are more likely to form the right mental model for what the community asks of them. The first experience sets the relational contract.

Market

Trade and gift with origin and impact cues.

The Market module applies a key insight from the trust finding: people are more likely to engage with a listing when they understand where the material came from and what giving it away or buying it means in circularity terms. Every listing includes provenance and impact fields. This is not optional metadata. It is the mechanism that turns commerce into a values-aligned exchange.

Community

A feed that makes reuse visible and social.

The Community module centres on DIY sharing and peer recognition. The design decision was to prioritise posts that show the before state — the raw material — alongside the outcome. This reduces the intimidation of a polished result and makes the making process itself the contribution. Users see that the gap between waste and creation is smaller than they assumed.

Ako

Short missions that build circular habits through guardianship values.

Ako is the learning module, but it was not designed as an education product. It was designed as a habit formation system. Missions are short, action-first and tied to the Koin recognition system. Completing a mission earns visible community standing, which connects personal learning to the relational value that Kaitiakitanga describes. The module closes the loop between knowing and doing.

Reflection

Looking back, looking forward.

Value

The project demonstrated that cultural framing can function as a motivation architecture rather than a visual add-on. The three-module structure held together through testing without needing significant structural revision.

Learned

Five usability participants is not enough to test a culturally situated product. Māori community input was referenced in the literature review but not embedded in the co-design process. That gap needs closing before any real deployment.

Next

A community pilot, genuine co-design with Māori knowledge holders, and longitudinal behaviour tracking to test whether the Koin system actually sustains circular habits rather than rewarding one-off engagement.
