Welnest

Helping newcomers find a home with clarity and confidence.

View Prototype Back to Works

Overview

A clearer way to search for a first home.

Welnest is a property search concept for newcomers settling into an unfamiliar city. Most listing platforms assume the user already understands local terms, trusts the agent network and can compare options confidently. New residents often have none of that context, so searching for a home becomes a source of stress rather than progress.

I treated the project as a trust-building problem before a search-interface problem. The work centred on plain-language listings, clearer comparison tools and a more transparent path to contacting an agent or booking an inspection.

Background

Renting in a new country starts with a trust problem, not a search problem.

Newcomer families arriving in Wellington face a housing market shaped by unfamiliar policies, inconsistent listing quality, and processes that assume local knowledge. Existing rental platforms and guidance resources are fragmented across government sites, agent listings and word-of-mouth advice, leaving little support for someone navigating all of it for the first time.

Problem

How might we help newcomers understand, compare and trust property listings, so finding a first home feels manageable rather than overwhelming?

Solution

Plain language, clear comparison, transparent next steps.

The solution rewrites listing terminology into plain language, adds a side-by-side comparison tool and makes contacting an agent or booking an inspection a visible, low-friction action rather than a hidden form.

Outcome

A search experience built around confidence, not just listings.

The final outcome is a covering search, comparison and inspection booking, refined through six rounds of . I would describe this as a strong course deliverable rather than a validated product. The next evidence needs to come from real newcomer testing outside a classroom setting.

Research

Newcomers face a trust gap, not just an information gap.

Research combined interviews with recent newcomers and a review of existing property platforms. The goal was to separate confusion caused by unfamiliar terminology from confusion caused by genuinely unclear interface design.

The user journey traced four stages: an early, cautious search; a confusing middle phase of comparing unfamiliar listings; a more confident shortlist once terms became clear; and a final step of reaching out, which only felt safe once the listing already felt understood.

Three findings stood out as the most useful for shaping scope.

Terminology

Listing terms assume local knowledge.

Words like "freehold" or "body corporate" were unfamiliar to most newcomers, who avoided listings rather than ask what the terms meant.

Comparison

Users compare listings in their head, not on screen.

Without a built-in way to compare options side by side, people lost track of details across tabs and screenshots.

Trust

Contacting an agent felt like a risk, not a next step.

Unclear contact flows made users hesitate, worried about looking uninformed or committing too early.

Influence

The journey map showed that trust had to be built before the comparison or contact steps, not after. This moved plain-language listings from a nice-to-have to the starting point of the whole design.

Design

Six principles guided every design decision.

The findings were translated into six design principles. These worked as filters for deciding what to simplify, what to keep and what needed clearer explanation on screen.

Transparency

Show what a listing means and where information comes from.

Clarity

Replace jargon with language a first-time buyer or renter understands.

Efficiency

Reduce the steps and screenshots needed to compare listings.

Accessibility

Keep contrast, hierarchy and reading order usable for all newcomers.

Supportiveness

Make the next step, like contacting an agent, feel safe to take.

Inclusiveness

Design for users with different language backgrounds and confidence levels.

Influence

These principles directly shaped the develop phase. Clarity and transparency drove the plain-language glossary, while supportiveness shaped how the contact and inspection actions were surfaced rather than buried in a form.

Validation

Six rounds of testing turned vague friction into specific fixes.

Each round of testing surfaced a specific usability issue, which was then translated into a targeted interface change.

Navigation Feedback

Users lost track of where they were in the search flow, so the interface needed clearer state and location cues.

Terminology Confusion

Property terms were still unclear to some testers, which led to inline plain-language explanations on key terms.

Rating Ambiguity

A listing quality score was misread as a guarantee rather than a guide, so the design added a short explanation of what the score covers.

Comparison Feature

Testers wanted to compare more than two listings at once, so the comparison view was redesigned to support a short shortlist.

Agent Contact

Some testers avoided the contact button, unsure what would happen next, so the flow now previews what the agent will see and how they will respond.

Inspection Report

Testers expected to see inspection details before booking, which led to a short inspection summary added ahead of the booking step.

Influence

Usability testing moved the project from a single round of polish to a genuinely iterative process. Each fix was scoped narrowly, which kept the prototype focused instead of trying to solve every possible issue at once.

Delivery

A search flow that explains itself as you go.

The final design carries the plain-language and supportiveness principles through two key moments: the listing comparison view and the agent contact step.

Comparison View

Compare listings side by side, in plain language.

The comparison view lets users hold two or three listings in view at once, with terminology already translated into plain language so the comparison itself does not require extra research.

Contact and Inspection

A visible, low-risk way to take the next step.

The contact flow previews what happens after a message is sent and surfaces an inspection summary before booking, turning a hesitant click into an informed decision.

Reflection

Looking back, looking forward.

Value

Reframing the problem around trust, not just information, drove every later decision. The design principles kept scope focused, and usability-driven fixes made the final flow genuinely tested rather than assumed.

Learned

Personalisation depth is shallow, and the prototype has not been validated with real newcomers outside a classroom testing setting.

Next

Next steps include defined usability metrics, diary studies with real newcomers, deeper personalisation and a dedicated accessibility review with analytics in place.
