Who's Singing OuTūīside?

A journey from garden to the sky

View Prototype

Overview

A short, immersive storybook that teaches the Tūī lifecycle through data, sound, and playful interaction.

This project translates a focused research topic into an interactive data-visual storytelling experience. I chose the Tūī as the subject and designed a narrative prototype for audiences aged 5+, combining infographics, clear navigation, and multimodal interaction (visual, audio, and tactile-like click/drag affordances) to teach natural science concepts quickly and memorably.

The work explores how data can become more readable and emotionally engaging when embedded in a child-led story structure, grounded in observation, accurate references, and iterative prototyping.

Problem

Nature learning is often either "clear" or "fun" — rarely both.

Children's nature education experiences can become either information-heavy (hard to follow) or entertainment-heavy (thin on learning). My challenge was to present a complex lifecycle (growth stages, predators, behaviour, sound, and cultural meaning) in a way that stays simple, navigable, and engaging, without overwhelming young learners or losing scientific clarity.

Solution

A story-driven, progressive-discovery data experience.

I designed an interactive storybook that frames learning through a young girl's perspective — a curiosity-driven narrative that guides users scene by scene — while using infographic visuals to anchor key facts (scale, time, proportions, and sound wave forms). The experience relies on progressive disclosure (revealing information through interaction), combines short text with clear visuals, and integrates sound/animation as sensory cues that support comprehension rather than distract from it.

Outcome

A hi-fi prototype that makes data feel readable and alive.

The final deliverable is a high-fidelity interactive prototype in Figma that packages the Tūī lifecycle into a cohesive journey — starting from hearing birdsong, discovering the bird, exploring life stages via tools (e.g., telescope/magnifier/headphones), and ending with a dreamlike "from garden to sky" closure. The project exceeded course expectation and received an A+, demonstrating a compelling method for presenting natural science knowledge through narrative data visualisation.

Design Process

From early audience evidence to a validated, multisensory story experience.

I began by using a short survey to set direction and shape an initial problem frame, then grounded the content through observation, museum reference, and research into Tūī biology, sound, predators, and cultural context. Next, I sketched and built three contrasting lo-fi narrative prototypes and ran comparative usability testing to identify what best supports clarity, engagement, and cultural comprehension. Those findings informed a hybrid direction and design principles, which guided refinement of the journey, IA, flows, and visual system before I delivered a polished hi-fi prototype with motion and sound.

Discover

Understanding what learners want and what the Tūī actually does.

I started with a short survey (11 responses) to understand baseline knowledge and preferred learning styles. Results showed people recognise Tūī but know little about its lifecycle, and strongly prefer story-led and interactive learning with links to cultural meaning. I used these signals to draft an initial problem frame and then grounded the content through field observation and secondary research on Tūī biology, sound, predators, and cultural context.

Background

I grew up in a city of concrete, fascinated by nature and wildlife. In New Zealand, birds live close to people, making nature feel approachable. The Tūī stands out with its striking plumage, diverse calls, and distinctive white throat tuft. This project lets me create an interactive Tūī experience for children aged five and above, while sharing creativity with my daughter.

Observation

Video and photo record

I recorded a kōwhai tree frequented by tūī and captured walk-by photos/clips to inform accurate food and activity scenes in the hi-fi prototype.

Secondary Research

Information

Nature, Bird, Tūī and Māori Culture

I mainly used research on New Zealand's native species to decide which one to feature, then studied the chosen bird in detail to understand its appearance, habits, and cultural links with Māori.

Secondary Research

Visualisation

Story, Color and Style

I explored Pinterest and children's books to study narrative logic, visual style, and color use in kids' literature. This helped guide the storytelling and visual design aspects of the project.

Define

Validating the concept through comparative prototyping.

I translated early framing into sketches and three lo-fi narrative prototypes (child story, timeline, learning-metaphor) and ran a 5-participant comparative usability test to evaluate clarity, navigation, engagement, interaction understanding, and cultural comprehension. Testing confirmed that story framing and progressive discovery increase engagement, but success depends on explicit navigation and clear labels — especially for predators and cultural symbolism.

Develop

Refining structure and craft into a cohesive experience.

I merged the best elements into one cohesive experience and tightened the journey, IA, and key flows. In parallel, I established a lightweight visual system — type hierarchy for readability, a palette inspired by the Tūī and its habitat, simple icons, and WCAG-aware choices (including 16px minimum body text) — then deepened the hi-fi across story, navigation, illustration consistency, interaction clarity, and data-vis (scale/proportion/sound wave cues).

Final Design

Child perspective storytelling, clear navigation, and multisensory cues.

The final hi-fi experience commits to the child-led narrative: the story begins with hearing the call, opening a curtain, and discovering the Tūī, then uses a light sidebar and tool metaphors (telescope/magnifier/headphones) to guide learning. Data visuals support measurable facts (scale, time, quantity, sound waves), while animations and audio cues heighten immersion.

Retrospective

Looking back, looking forward.

Value

This project demonstrated that narrative framing can dramatically change comprehension and motivation. Embedding data within a child-led story structure made scientific information more readable, emotionally resonant, and memorable than a conventional infographic format.

Learnings

Ambiguity in labels (especially around predators and cultural symbolism) quickly erodes clarity. Future iterations would invest more in microcopy, contextual hints, and controllable audio. Hybridising prototypes — borrowing strengths rather than forcing one format — was the most productive design move of the project.

Next Steps

  • Further usability testing on story readability, interaction effectiveness, and navigation clarity.
  • Expand content into regional distribution, population trends, and conservation status.
  • Explore a more continuous "one-shot" immersive narrative, potentially adding video or interactive 3D models.

HENEX Method Recipe

R16 explains how meaning before data made the Tūī lifecycle readable for young learners.

Primary recipe R16 · Why First Data Story Perception × Meaning × Medium

This project uses R16 because every design decision prioritised emotional and narrative meaning before presenting raw data. The birdsong hook, the child-led story perspective, and the dreamlike ending all establish meaning first — so when lifecycle facts (egg size, predator proportions, sound wave patterns) appear, learners already have a reason to care about them. Data became more readable precisely because it was embedded in a story that had already answered "why does this matter?"
